## Q2: does the new long rule contradict `_common_rules.jinja2` in a way a user would see?

With the precedence line gone, nothing says which rule set wins. Conflicts go back to being decided by the model, as they were at `c5ee843`. The new line 21 is a positional and more specific instruction ("Item 1 is ..."), so where it collides with a type note, it is likely to win.

- **Narrative (anime, drama, novel, film).** C:46 says "do not move the ending to the beginning". T:21 says item 1 is what the author "decides or concludes". For a story, "the source's conclusion" can easily be read as how the story ends. Expected output for a drama episode or film: item 1 states the outcome ("最終的に主人公は…と決断する"), and the other items are facts from earlier in the story. The rule defines the conclusion as something someone "rates, recommends, decides or concludes", and the fallback ("if the source has no conclusion") gives the model a way out, but "decides or concludes" fits a plot resolution too. **This is worse than before.** At the parent, the long rule exempted narratives from importance order and the precedence line backed the type note, so both rules agreed on source order. Now the two rules contradict each other, and the more specific one points at the ending. Finding 1.
- **Procedural (recipe, tutorial).** C:39 says "do not reorder steps". T:21-22 put a verdict first ("the host says it is ready in 10 minutes and recommends it for lunch boxes") and then "the facts that matter most". Steps are not features, figures, prices or events, so the likely output has no steps at all: a verdict, then quantities, times and storage periods. Any steps that do appear are most likely in source order, because C:39 still applies to them. Compared with before: the parent kept the source order for procedures, so its key points probably read as a condensed walkthrough. The new output is a verdict plus specs. This is a different output, not a broken one. A user reading key points for a recipe is not misled. It is not worse in a way that reads as a bug, so it is not recorded as a finding. It belongs in the R-5 check: does a recipe's item 1 invent a verdict? See finding 1's note on T:26.
- **Multi-topic sources (C:2-7).** "Item 1 is the source's conclusion" assumes there is one conclusion. For a video with several recipes or products, the model may write one combined verdict that blends topics, which is the cross-topic bleed C:6 forbids. Expected output: something like "the host recommends both A and B" when the source recommended only A. This is a minor variant of finding 1's mechanism, and the R-5 set should check it if it contains a multi-product video. It is folded into finding 1.
- **Documentary (report, thesis, contract).** C:65 says to follow the chapter structure "as closely as possible". T:21 puts the conclusion first. Expected output: the thesis or report conclusion first, then the remaining points roughly in chapter order. A contract has no one who rates or recommends, so the fallback applies and the most important clause comes first. This is closer to the spec's intent (most important first outside procedures and narratives) than the parent, where the precedence line returned reports to chapter order (r2 finding 2a). **Not worse.** No finding.
- **Informational (news).** C:58 gives `"reported that ..."` as the way to mark unconfirmed information, and that is an agentless form. T:23 now names "the outlet that reported it" as a valid holder, which gives the model a named alternative. That is better than the parent, where the precedence line made the type note win (r2 finding 1). However, T:23 covers only "opinion, rating, recommendation or forecast". An unconfirmed leak or a disputed allegation is none of these, so the passive ban does not reach it, and C:58's agentless example is the only instruction that does. Expected output: "新型は春に発売されると報じられている" for a leak, with no outlet named. Compared with the parent, the outcome is about the same (agentless either way), but the cause has moved from the precedence line to the narrowed scope of T:23. Finding 2.
- **Conversational (interview, roundtable).** C:49 says "Always identify whose statement it is" and C:50 says to preserve the opposing structure. T:22 says to "State facts plainly". When one guest asserts a disputed factual claim ("this drug has no side effects") and another disputes it, T:23 does not cover the claim (it is not an opinion, rating, recommendation or forecast), so T:22 invites stating it as fact. The parent's long rule listed "disputed claims" among the things to attribute. Finding 2.
- **Evaluative (review), the production case.** T:21 and C:53 agree. This is the case the commit was measured on (verdict first in 9 of 11). No conflict.

- **Type-precedence line (added in `7ea5ab6` for r1 finding 5).** Removing it closes r2 finding 1 (the Informational note's agentless example winning over the passive ban) and r2 finding 2b (a review told as a story, classed as Narrative, kept in source order with the verdict last). It reopens r1 finding 5's three conflicts as conflicts with no stated winner:

  - Conversational and Informational are partly covered by T:23's "the speaker's name, or the outlet that reported it", except for unconfirmed and disputed factual claims (finding 2).

  It also opens one conflict that r1 and r2 never had to address, because the exemption covered it: Narrative (finding 1).
- **Procedures/narratives source-order exemption (from the spec, `c5ee843`).** This exemption is what kept narratives safe. Removing it is the cause of finding 1. For procedures, see Q2: the output is different but not misleading.
- **"Do not start an item by naming the source ('This video ...', 'The article ...')".** r1 confirmed this rule as part of what the spec asked for. Nothing in r1 or r2 shows it causing a problem, so dropping it is not a response to any record. T:21 now asks item 1 to state "who says it". When the source has no clearly named speaker (a narrated explainer, an unsigned article), the nearest available subject is the source itself, and the model is likely to produce "この動画では…と結論づけている" or "The video concludes that ...". That is exactly the opener the removed rule banned. It is also an attribution to no person, which is close in kind to what T:23 forbids. Finding 3.
- **"disputed claims" dropped from the attribution list.** This is a quiet removal inside the rewrite of the facts/opinions block. It is finding 2.
- **"Unattributed evaluations may be mistaken for the summarizer's opinion" and "to save characters".** These are rationale clauses with no rule content. Nothing reopens.
